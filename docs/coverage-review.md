# Where the learning journey stands

Reviewed October 20, 2026. Math Atlas now has **271 distinct adventure missions, 813 challenges, and 120 teaching concept families**. The original **80-lesson visual library** remains available alongside the adventure.

Connected journeys now run from Kindergarten through Grade 5. This expansion adds **32 original lessons each for Grades 4 and 5**: 64 lessons and 192 challenges. Each new activity includes a visual introduction, independent worked example, explanation prompt, and planned review. The earlier K–3 journeys remain available.

## What the connected early years provide

The [primary-years guide](primary-years.md) lays out six units per grade. The [Grade 3 guide](grade-3-year.md) and [Grades 4–5 guide](upper-elementary-years.md) lay out eight per grade. Each year offers about 36 flexible study weeks, short worked examples, prerequisites, explanation prompts, and suggested review. These budgets help a family revisit school topics; they are not 180 daily lesson plans.

The learning progression moves from objects and actions to pictures, quantities, and symbols. Kindergarten joins and removes small collections. Grade 1 develops addition and subtraction strategies and tens. Grade 2 extends bundles, measurement, time, data, arrays, and equal shares. Grade 3 develops multiplication, division, and fractions as numbers. Grades 4–5 extend place value, calculation, fractions, decimal notation and operations, measurement, geometry, volume, coordinates, and expressions. Correctly finishing a short mission records practice, not mastery of a grade or standard.

Continue deepening these years with longer discussions, additional representations, and problems that do not closely resemble the worked example. Kindergarten still needs more practice with the counting sequence through 100, numeral writing away from a screen, and varied number combinations. Grades 1–2 need sustained untimed arithmetic practice, more varied unknown positions and measurement situations, and classroom transfer. Grade 3 needs continued review and richer applications rather than only larger inventories of similar questions.

## Build upward in this order

| Priority | Main gaps to fill | A useful visual direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grades 6–8 | Complete rational-number, expression, equation, geometry, statistics, and probability paths with review | Link number lines, balance models, tables, graphs, shape transformations, and data experiments |
| High school | Connected course paths, proof, modeling, broader symbolic work, and statistics | Connect equations with graphs and geometric or data-based reasons; retain mature presentation options |
| College | Foundations and prerequisites before expanding the current sampler | Make precise model assumptions and connect a picture with its formal notation |

The Grade 4 and Grade 5 paths draw on their official [Grade 4 overview](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/4/introduction/) and [Grade 5 overview](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/5/introduction/). They need deeper varied applications, sustained written-method practice, more mixed-number situations, geometric constructions, and fresh examples that require explanation without closely following a worked card. Middle-school planning references are the [Grade 6](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/6/introduction/), [Grade 7](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/7/introduction/), and [Grade 8](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/8/introduction/) overviews.

## Evidence to require as content grows

- State the learning goal, prerequisite, worked reasoning, and what each visible quantity counts.
- Introduce an unfamiliar idea before checking an answer; keep hints and adult guidance available.
- Verify answers against independent worked examples and check that controls preserve the intended mathematics.
- Check keyboard, narrow-screen, reading-support, and reduced-motion use. Young learners should not need typing to demonstrate an idea.
- Include varied contexts and representations, then ask whether the learner can explain a fresh example.
- Record exact source editions and licenses for adaptations. The new 64 activities are original; the four existing credited IM adaptations are unchanged.

These are development and review criteria. They do not imply educator endorsement, verified state-by-state alignment, or a particular publisher's lesson order. Teacher accounts, classroom tracking, and voice narration remain outside the current student-first scope.
