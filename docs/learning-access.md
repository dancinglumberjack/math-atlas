# Built around different ways of learning

Math Atlas is a visual learning project for children and families, including learners who use ADHD, dyslexia, and other reading supports. These design choices aim to reduce barriers; they are not a diagnosis, treatment, a guarantee of outcomes, or a certification of accessibility.

## Everyday design choices

- Start with clear sans-serif type, larger everyday text, spacious lines, and one worked step at a time. Existing personal choices are kept. No diagnosis or preset selection is needed to get this baseline.
- Introduce a new concept with a picture and a small example before asking a question about it.
- Keep one clear goal at a time. Use short, concrete instructions and consistent navigation.
- Let a learner build, move, compare, or choose before requiring written notation.
- Pair color with labels, shape, or position. Keep the mathematical relationships true.
- Keep hints, explanations, retries, pauses, and reading preferences available without losing coins or access.
- Preserve a learner's work and resume position in the same browser whenever storage is available.
- Make celebrations brief and connected to an action. Provide a quieter view and respect reduced-motion settings.
- Do not add background narration, countdown pressure, limited lives, public rankings, or pet-care penalties.
- Use keyboard alternatives and large primary controls. Keep focus stable when interactive objects change.
- Let a parent guide younger children. The activities do not assume an early learner can independently read instructions.

The stage picker chooses a topic and presentation. It does not infer ability, a diagnosis, or an actual age. Reading controls remain personal choices; learners can combine them in whichever way feels useful.

## Guidance behind the choices

The project uses [W3C's cognitive accessibility guidance on helping users focus](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/objectives/o5-user-focus/) and [clear steps and controls](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/) as design references. These are supplemental guidance, not a claim that the whole site meets WCAG.

[CAST's Universal Design for Learning framework](https://www.cast.org/what-we-do/universal-design-for-learning/) informs offering different ways to engage with an idea, represent it, and act or respond. The activities still need continued testing with learners, parents, educators, keyboard users, and assistive-technology users.
