# A year of Grade 3 discoveries

Math Atlas now has a connected Grade 3 study map: eight units, 76 short lessons, and planned opportunities to return to earlier ideas. The planning budget is about 36 flexible weeks. A week is a guide for spreading out learning, not a deadline.

This is a growing companion to school instruction and adult guidance. A completed mission records practice, including practice with help. It does not establish mastery or certify coverage of a school’s full course. One short mission is not a complete day’s mathematics lesson.

In the app, choose Grade 3 and all topics to follow the units. A topic filter can help with tonight’s homework. Any unit remains available; useful prerequisites are suggestions to revisit, rather than admission tests.

Units have different numbers of short lessons. Allow more time for physical building, drawing, discussion, a fresh example, and revisiting an idea that remains uncertain. The eight budgets add to 36 weeks; they can stretch or shrink around the school’s calendar and the learner’s needs.

## A simple rhythm across several sessions

Start by reopening one familiar picture or recalling an earlier example. Keep hints and reading adjustments available. Then work through a new concept’s teaching cards before asking the learner to answer a question.

On another session, build the same relationship with counters, paper strips, a clock, a drawing, or the app’s controls. Connect the objects to the numbers and symbols. Ask what each quantity means.

Try a fresh application when the learner is ready. Later, return to one of the unit’s review missions and explain what is remembered and what support is useful. A correction can be the work for that session. There is no timer, lost streak, or penalty for help.

Every unit lists three review missions in the year metadata. Some revisit an earlier unit; others revisit an idea first encountered near the start of the current unit. These are practice suggestions, not a separate reward schedule.

## What deeper work can look like

### Unit 3: show the value behind a written calculation

For **268 + 157**, build or sketch the place values first. Eight ones plus seven ones make fifteen ones: exchange ten of them for one ten and keep five ones. The tens are now six plus five plus the exchanged ten: twelve tens. Exchange ten tens for one hundred. The result is four hundreds, two tens, and five ones: **425**.

For **403 − 178**, exchange one hundred for ten tens. Exchange one of those tens for ten ones. The same 403 is now three hundreds, nine tens, and thirteen ones. Subtract one hundred, seven tens, and eight ones to get **225**. Check with **178 + 225 = 403**.

The written record should show the same trades. A learner can also use a number line, expanded form, or another correct strategy and explain how the quantities connect.

### Unit 4: make the division question visible

Put **18 counters on three plates**, sharing equally. Each plate gets six counters. The answer is **six counters per plate**.

Now put the same **18 counters into bags holding three each**. Six bags are needed. The answer is **six bags**. The calculation 18 ÷ 3 = 6 appears in both stories, while the unknown quantity has a different meaning.

Build both versions. Point to the whole, the number of groups, and the size of a group. Then connect the related facts:

- 3 × 6 = 18 and 6 × 3 = 18.
- 18 ÷ 3 = 6 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.

For a useful multiplication strategy, split **6 × 8** into **5 × 8 + 1 × 8 = 40 + 8 = 48**. No object is added by the split, and none belongs to both parts.

For division, decompose a friendly whole: **84 ÷ 4 = (80 ÷ 4) + (4 ÷ 4) = 20 + 1 = 21**. Each decomposed part is still shared among the same four groups. These paper examples extend the app’s bounded examples; they are not an instruction to enter a larger number into a control that does not support it.

In a two-step story, name the result of each step. If six bags contain four snacks each and two whole bags leave, **four bags remain**. Then **4 × 4 = 16 snacks remain**. Subtracting two snacks from the original total would describe a different story.

### Unit 5: teach the whole before the fraction name

Begin with one paper strip. Split it into **eight equal-sized parts**. Color three. The top number in **3/8** counts the selected parts; the bottom number counts all equal parts in that one whole. Show unequal pieces too: counting three pieces does not describe three eighths unless the whole was divided into eight equal parts.

Move to a number line. Keep **zero to one** as one whole unit. Split that distance into four equal intervals. Each interval is one quarter. Three such intervals reach **3/4**. Continue with the same interval size beyond one: five intervals reach **5/4**.

Compare fractions using the same-sized whole. Three eighths is greater than one eighth because it contains more of the same-sized parts. One third is greater than one sixth because each third is larger. Do not compare denominator digits without considering the whole and the parts.

### Unit 6: read units and intervals before calculating

A clock reading is a position in time; elapsed time is a duration. From **2:40 to 3:15**, move twenty minutes to 3:00 and another fifteen minutes to 3:15. The duration is **35 minutes**.

Read a scale’s unit and interval size before its marked value. Two liters plus three liters is five liters. A mass of 350 grams plus 200 grams is 550 grams. The numbers are attached to units; container height alone does not establish liquid volume.

For a line plot, measure several paper lengths to the nearest half or quarter inch. Put one mark above each measurement, including repeated measurements. Count marks to count objects; read the horizontal location to identify length. Those are different quantities.

## Connecting this with school homework

Use the topic, the quantities, and the teacher’s current method to choose a matching activity. Ask the learner to point to where each number appears in the picture. Keep a model available while connecting it with a written calculation.
